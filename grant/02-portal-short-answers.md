## Rhabdomyosarcoma relevance

Limit: 150 words.

This project studies only fusion-positive rhabdomyosarcoma, the subtype driven by PAX3-FOXO1 and the group of children most likely to relapse with metastatic disease. The question is whether that fusion promotes growth and spread through one heparan sulfate proteoglycan, SPOCK3. SPOCK3 is the molecular target. The mechanism under test is capture of the growth factor FGF2, activation of ERK, and cortical stiffness that helps cells pass through narrow spaces during extravasation. The models are fusion-positive cells already in use in this laboratory, including cells in which PAX3-FOXO1 has been knocked down. Fusion-negative cells are a comparison. The tool compound is surfen, an antagonist of heparan sulfate. The result this award is built to deliver is whether blocking SPOCK3, or heparan sulfate more broadly, is a credible way to slow growth and metastatic spread in this cancer.

## Data, resource sharing, and collaboration

Limit: 200 words.

Once the SPOCK3 knockout line is validated, it will be shared with academic rhabdomyosarcoma laboratories under a Children’s Hospital Los Angeles material-transfer agreement. Protocols for expansion microscopy, atomic force microscopy of cortical tension, and the narrow-pore transwell assay will be posted as step-by-step methods. Quantified measurements of heparan sulfate abundance, FGF2 capture, ERK activation, proliferation, cortical tension, and pore passage will be released with the paper, together with the notes required to reproduce them. Any new RNA or protein data will be deposited in a public repository when the paper is published or within 12 months after the award ends, whichever comes first. Each year we will give the Rhabdo Coalition a short, nontechnical update that member organizations can share with families. This project does not generate individual patient-level data.

## Funding leverage and impact

Limit: 150 words.

The laboratory already has fusion-positive models and a PAX3-FOXO1 knockdown system. Preliminary experiments already connect that knockdown to lower SPOCK3, weaker FGF2 capture, lower ERK activity, slower proliferation, a softer cell cortex, and less passage through narrow pores. Those observations are not yet a finished test of SPOCK3 itself. Coalition funding pays for the SPOCK3 knockout, expansion microscopy, the surfen experiments, and the atomic-force and transwell measurements that ask whether SPOCK3 is required. Without this support, the finding stays preliminary. With it, one shared review can give member foundations a direct yes-or-no answer on whether SPOCK3 and heparan sulfate are worth pursuing to slow growth and spread in fusion-positive rhabdomyosarcoma.
