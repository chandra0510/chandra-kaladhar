# Project overview and lay summary

Limit: 250 words. Paste the paragraph below.

Rhabdomyosarcoma is the most common soft-tissue cancer in children (~350 U.S. cases/year). Fusion-positive disease is hardest to cure when it spreads or returns, and intensive chemotherapy leaves lasting harm. It is driven by an abnormal "joined gene," PAX3-FOXO1—two genes stuck together that keep the cancer growing. These tumors often form a honeycomb alveolar pattern that may shelter cells from treatment; fusion-negative disease lacks this pattern. No medicine turns the fusion gene off, so we must find other weaknesses.

Lab tests usually grow these cells on flat dishes or simple gels unlike real muscle, so drugs that look strong in dishes can fail in tissue. We found that fusion-positive cells need a growth-control protein called WEE1 on dishes. WEE1-blocking drugs already exist and are being tested in other cancers. This project asks whether that dependence still holds—and where in the tumor—when cells grow in a muscle-like setting closer to childhood disease.

We built scaffolds from decellularized skeletal muscle that keep natural structure and allow clear microscopy—a shareable model to test WEE1 drugs in tissue more like a real tumor. We will grow fusion-positive cells there, compare them with patient and animal-model tumors and with dish and gel cultures, then map which cells divide, are stressed, or survive treatment. We will share the model and results with Rhabdo Coalition labs.

For families, this clarifies whether WEE1 inhibition should be prioritized for children, or whether architecture protects some cells and combination therapy is needed—so next steps rest on tissue-relevant evidence, not dish-only results.
