# Significance and impact

Limit: one page. Paste everything under this line.

Children with fusion-positive rhabdomyosarcoma relapse too often, and relapse after metastatic disease is rarely curable. PAX3-FOXO1 defines this subtype and drives much of its biology, but the fusion protein has not become a medicine. Treatment is still chemotherapy, surgery, and radiation, largely shared with fusion-negative disease. A useful next target is a molecule the fusion controls, one that sits on a pathway a drug can reach, and one that participates in growth or in the mechanical step of leaving the tumor.

Heparan sulfate proteoglycans are candidates of that kind. On the cell surface they bind growth factors, including FGF2, and present them to receptor tyrosine kinases. That presentation is a known route to ERK signaling and proliferation. The same class of molecules can also change the cell surface and the cell cortex, so they are plausible regulators of how stiff a cell is and how readily it deforms through a narrow pore. Extravasation requires that deformation. If PAX3-FOXO1 maintains a particular proteoglycan, then growth-factor capture and cortical stiffness could be two reads of one fusion-dependent program, and both could be interrupted without drugging the fusion itself.

The preliminary work points to SPOCK3. Knockdown of PAX3-FOXO1 lowers SPOCK3, reduces heparan sulfate displayed for growth-factor binding, and weakens FGF2 capture. FGF2-driven ERK activation and proliferation fall with it. The same knockdown cells appear less stiff, and they are less able to pass through narrow pores. That is a coherent trail from the fusion gene to a surface molecule to two behaviors families care about: the tumor keeping growing, and cells getting out. It is not yet proof that SPOCK3 is the responsible molecule. Other proteoglycans could carry the phenotype, and the stiffness result is still described as an appearance rather than a completed measurement. The project is the experiment that distinguishes those possibilities.

Aim 1 removes SPOCK3 and asks whether heparan sulfate display, FGF2 capture, ERK, and proliferation depend on it. Surfen, a small-molecule antagonist of heparan sulfate, asks the same question pharmacologically. Aim 2 asks whether SPOCK3 and surfen change cortical tension and pore passage. Surfen is a tool, not a claim that it is ready for children. If SPOCK3 knockout copies the PAX3-FOXO1-knockdown phenotype, SPOCK3 becomes a defined target downstream of the fusion. If knockout does nothing and surfen still works, the relevant target is heparan sulfate more broadly, and SPOCK3 was a marker rather than the driver. If neither intervention works, this path is a poor bet and should not be carried toward therapy.

Either result is useful to the laboratories and family foundations that have to choose which rhabdomyosarcoma ideas to fund. The longer path, which this award does not claim to finish, is a treatment aimed at this surface pathway in fusion-positive disease. The award’s job is to show whether that path is real.
